The instrument, in two sizes

The CONFIG screen — and the first field on the front page — switches between them. Nothing is deleted when you switch down: a modulation fed by a module the smaller machine does not show is bypassed rather than thrown away, and it comes back when you switch up again.

BENJOLIN — 17 screens

The original instrument, and the reason the rest exists. Two oscillators run into a comparator that asks one question — is A above B — and the edges of that answer are the machine's only sense of time. There is no clock. Tune the oscillators against each other and the rhythm changes, because the rhythm is the tuning.

  • OSC-1, OSC-2 — each tuned as a whole-number ratio against the root, with a fine detune in cents. The ratio is the important control: simple ratios lock, awkward ones wander.
  • CHAOS-A in RUNGLER mode — a shift register clocked by one oscillator and fed by the other. No random source anywhere, which is why a groove you find stays found. STEPS sets how long the loop is, CHANCE how often a new bit gets in: at 0 the figure repeats forever, at 100 every clock takes fresh data.
  • COMPARATOR — the time base, and a voice in its own right. OFFSET slides B under A, which is pulse width and therefore the rhythm. OUT picks what comes out of the audio jack without touching the timing: a hard square, soft or hard clipping, a wavefolder, a rectifier, or analogue min/max.
  • FILTER — the comparator's pulse train through a resonant multimode filter, swept by the rungler. This is where the benjolin's voice actually comes from.

Start here: put the cursor on RATE on the front page and hold D or C. It moves both oscillators together, so the whole machine slides between a slow tick and a scream without the pattern changing.

ADVANCED — 24 screens

The same core with a conventional studio built around it. The comparator still runs, and you can still take everything from it — but now there is a choice.

  • CLOCK — a real tempo, in BPM, with two dividers off it. This is the one part of the machine that keeps time on its own, and it exists only here. Its base pulse is a sixteenth note; the dividers are named where they land on one (/4 is a quarter, /16 a bar).
  • SEQ-1, SEQ-2 — eight steps, four banks of eight patterns, each clocked from whatever gate you point it at. They ship deliberately split: SEQ-1 on the clock, SEQ-2 on the comparator, so you can hear a line that keeps time against one that does not.
  • DRUMS — kick, snare, closed and open hat. The first sub-page is the routing: each voice picks a trigger, a division of it, and a chance of actually firing. The other two hold the voice parameters.
  • CHAOS-B — a second chaos oscillator, so the two can modulate different things.

Anything that takes a trigger can take it from anywhere: the clock, either divider, either comparator edge, an oscillator, or a rungler shift. That list is the hinge between the two machines — point the drums at the clock and it is a drum machine, point them at the comparator and it is a benjolin with percussion.

The effects, and where a voice meets them

Six stages in a chain, and every voice chooses where it joins.

DIRTdrive, bit crushing, decimation, and four shapes — soft clipping, an asymmetric one that adds even harmonics, a clip-crush-fold stack, and a ring-modulated wrecker
FXone swept delay at four lengths: phaser, flanger, chorus, ensemble
GLITCHbeat repeat. A gate decides when a slice is grabbed and CHANCE decides whether it is. LEN is in milliseconds, or SYNC, which takes the length from the gap between the gate's own pulses — point the gate at the clock and a slice is a note value
GRAINoverlapping windowed reads of the same one-second buffer GLITCH uses
DELAYfour taps off one line, each with its own time, level and position
SPACEreverb, in three characters: a plain room, a shimmer that feeds a pitched copy back into itself, and a gated one

On MIX each voice has a third column naming where it joins: ALL for the whole chain, or FX, GLITCH, DELAY, SPACE to skip everything before that point, or DRY to skip all of it. Drums clean but in the same room as everything else is DELAY; a comparator that takes the distortion and nothing after it is DIRT with the distortion moved last.

CHAIN, the second sub-page of MIX, is the order itself. The shipped one is the conventional one — drive first because distorting a reverb tail turns it to mud, time effects last, delay before reverb — and it is a setting because the placements that argument does not settle are worth having. The looper before the delay repeats a dry slice and lets the delay answer it; after, it repeats the tail as well.

Modulation

Most screens carry a bank of rows underneath their own controls. Each row is a source, a bipolar amount, and a destination inside that module, so Space drops a row in and out without losing what it was set to. Eight signals are available everywhere: both chaos oscillators, both audio oscillators, both sequencers, the comparator, and the clock.

Full travel on any of them is ±10 V in the machine's own terms — twenty octaves on a pitch destination — so a little goes a long way and the interesting settings are usually small.

One C++ codebase, built for the browser and for an M5Stack Cardputer. The panel above is the same 240×135 screen, the same engine, at the same sample rate.
